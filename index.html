Cyber-Informed Engineering

A plain-language description of the new SP Clarifier® treatment train, followed by a Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) review: start from what must never happen to the water, then make sure the design itself, not only the network, prevents it.

Built from 14 project documents: P&IDs, control philosophy, control logic and sequence chart (CLSC), process description, panel schematics and panel layout. This is a draft engineering discussion, not a certified security assessment.

Biggest exposure

Chemical dosing (by others) has no hardwired limit shown in the drawings. It is the easiest route to harmful water.

Best existing design

Fail-open vent valves, one blowdown valve at a time, local-only compressors and a mechanical vacuum bleed valve.

What CIE changes

Same attacker, already inside: network-only defense scores 51/100 (serious); the full CIE design scores 5/100. See the simulation.

1 · The project

What is being built

The City of Stilwell is adding a high-rate clarification stage to its surface-water plant. The equipment supplier provides two SuperPulsator® (SP) Clarifier trains that remove turbidity, color and organics before the water moves on to filtration. Pumping, chemical feed, the rapid-mix tank, the sludge sump and all interconnecting piping are by others.

5.0 MGDtotal capacity, 2 trains × 2.5 MGD (1,736 gpm each)
≤ 2.0 NTUclarifier effluent turbidity, 95% of measurements
10pneumatic valves: 2 vent + 8 blowdown
3vacuum pumps (5 HP each): 1 per train + 1 shared spare
2air compressors, 175 psig, 120 gal receiver
58′×20′×18′concrete basin per train, 1.5 gpm/ft² loading

Process flow

Dashed boxes are supplied by others. The key idea: a vacuum pump pulls water up into a chamber, a vent valve releases it, and the surge makes a deep sludge blanket gently pulse. Settling plates finish the separation, and four blowdown valves per train periodically draw excess sludge off by gravity, one valve at a time. There is no mechanical mixer under water.

2 · Controls

How it is automated

Panel and PLC

One UL 508A, NEMA 4X panel on 120 VAC with UPS. Allen-Bradley CompactLogix controller with distributed I/O, an Ethernet switch and a touchscreen HMI. Selector switches give local/remote/off on each vacuum pump and off/semi-auto/auto blowdown per train.

Train states

OFF, STANDBY and PRODUCTION. In PRODUCTION the vent valve cycles (default 30 s closed, 20 s open) while the PLC waits for a blowdown trigger by time, by flow volume or by operator button. Blowdown runs a 9-step sequence, one valve open at a time.

Plant PLC link

Nine signals in (feed flow, turbidity, pH, mixer status and a permissive to blowdown) and about thirty out (valve and pump feedback, callout). Both PLCs exchange a heartbeat; a stalled heartbeat for 5 s raises COMMUNICATION FAILURE.

Built-in safe states

  • Vent valves must fail open
  • Power loss: valves to fail position, motors stop, trains go to Faulted step
  • Mechanical System Stop pushbutton removes power to outputs
  • Alarms for forced I/O and program reloaded from flash
3 · Cyber-Informed Engineering

Start from consequences, not from the network

CIE (developed at Idaho National Laboratory and now part of U.S. national strategy) asks engineers to treat cyber risk like any other design hazard: identify the worst outcomes, then use physical, mechanical and process limits so that even a fully compromised controller cannot produce them. Network defenses still matter, but they become one layer rather than the whole plan.

1 · Consequence prioritization

List the events that would harm public health or the plant, and rank them by severity, not by how likely an intrusion seems.

2 · System-of-systems analysis

Trace what each consequence needs: which PLC outputs, which signals from others, which utilities (air, power), which people.

3 · Consequence-based targeting

Think like the attacker: find the shortest, least-defended path to each high-consequence event, using the dependencies you mapped.

4 · Mitigations and protections

Break each path with engineering first (passive, hardwired, fail-safe), then add detection and recovery, and rehearse running the plant by hand.

Consequence scenarios (tap to open)

A · Coagulant, alkalinity or polymer dosed wrongly Highest consequence
Consequence
Floc fails, turbidity breaks through toward filters and distribution, or pH swings out of range.
Path
Chemical feed is by others, but the clarifier depends on it. Feed pH and turbidity (04-AIT-241/242) are monitored with alarms; nothing in the clarifier drawings stops a chemical pump.
Design it out
Hardwired high/low pH and turbidity interlocks at the chemical feed skid, metering pumps with fixed maximum stroke or capacity, and tank or day-tank volumes sized so a worst-case overfeed cannot reach harmful dose.
B · Pulsing disrupted (vent valve or vacuum pumps) High
Consequence
Sludge blanket loses uniformity, solids carry over into the clarified water.
Path
Software can stop the pumps, hold the vent valve closed or change the open/closed timers (ranges 5–60 s and 15–60 s).
Already good
Vent valve fails open, a vacuum breaker is on the P&ID, vacuum level is set by a mechanical bleed valve at the pump suction, and a spare pump with manual valve alignment exists.
Design it out
Lock or car-seal the bleed valve and manual pump valves; keep timer limits tight; add a hardwired high-vacuum limit; train operators on hand-mode pulsing.
C · Blowdown abused: too much, too little, or all at once High
Consequence
Sludge sump overflow, wasted water, or blanket overgrowth and carry-over if blowdown is blocked.
Already good
Only one valve open at a time and one clarifier at a time in logic; the concentrator design cannot withdraw blanket slurry; a plant-PLC permissive gates blowdown.
Design it out
Enforce one-valve-at-a-time with hardwired relay interlocks, not only logic; hardwired sump high-level inhibit; size piping and orifices so full-open flow cannot exceed what the sump can take; make the permissive a hardwired contact.
D · Falsified or blocked plant-PLC data High
Consequence
Fake feed flow starts or stops trains; fake pH or turbidity hides a real problem; a spoofed permissive triggers blowdown at the wrong time.
Path
Ethernet link carries flow, quality and permissive signals. The heartbeat is a simple counter, and the philosophy suggests continuing to run on comms loss.
Design it out
Plausibility and rate-of-change checks, hardwire the few signals that gate safety, use authenticated and allow-listed industrial protocols, and write down the deliberate comms-loss fail state for each function.
E · Loss of compressed air or power Largely covered
Consequence
Valves cannot move; trains fault.
Already good
Compressors run only locally (not network-reachable), receiver has a relief valve set at 200 psig, valves go to defined positions, UPS rides through short events.
Suggestion
Publish one table of fail positions per valve (note 4 on the P&IDs is ambiguous) and verify it in factory and site acceptance tests.
F · HMI and remote access misuse High likelihood
Consequence
Anyone with HMI access could change setpoints, modes or valve states.
Path
The control philosophy documents default accounts for each role, notes there is no limit on password attempts, and encourages (but does not require) changing them.
Design it out
Make unique credentials and attempt lockout a commissioning gate; separate operator, supervisor and engineer roles; put vendor and remote access behind a controlled gateway with multi-factor login and logging. Keep the default credentials out of any shared copy of the documents.

Engineered limits (CIE)

Lesson: with network-only defense, one stolen login leads to a serious outcome. With the engineered limits, the same attacker can still change settings, but the water, the sump and the chemical dose stay inside safe bounds. That is consequence reduction by design.

6 · The four phases applied

Consequence-driven CIE, phase by phase

INL’s consequence-driven CIE method runs in four phases. Here each one is applied to the clarifier trains. The ratings and path counts are my engineering judgment from the drawings, for discussion with the design team.

Phase 1 · Consequence prioritization

Decide which events matter most. Criteria: public health, loss of service, environment, equipment damage.

RankHigh-consequence eventWhy it ranks here
1Out-of-spec water (high turbidity, wrong pH or coagulant excess) leaves the clarifiersPublic-health risk; the clarifier is the first barrier for solids and organics

3Sludge sump overflow or uncontrolled dischargeEnvironmental and compliance impact; sump is by others
4Equipment damage (vacuum pump run dry, air receiver overpressure)Costly and slow to repair, but limited public impact
